Drafting a Master Plan does not start with writing a bill — it starts by organizing who decides and by reading the territory you intend to plan honestly. This post walks through the process from start to finish, split into five stages, following the methodology of Brazil's *Guide for Drafting and Revising Master Plans*.

![Urban zoning map overlaid on a georeferenced territorial grid](/assets/images/plano_diretor_cover2.webp)

In Brazil, the Master Plan (*Plano Diretor*) is the municipal law that guides urban development and expansion policy. It organizes the territory, ensures that property fulfills its social and environmental function, and is the main mechanism for delivering the right to sustainable cities — housing, infrastructure, sanitation, and mobility. I have already written about [why it relies directly on surveying and mapping](/en/2026/05/28/plano-diretor-topografia.html); here the focus is the drafting process itself.

The plan does not end with itself. It unfolds into instruments that make it work day to day: **Macrozoning** and **Zoning**, **Land Subdivision** laws, inducement instruments such as Special Zones of Social Interest (ZEIS) and charged building rights, plus sector plans for mobility, sanitation, and housing. Each stage below sets the ground so those instruments are chosen with judgment.

## Stage 1 — Preparation: build governance before the diagnosis

Before any diagnosis, you have to define who runs the process. The recommendation is to form a **steering group** led by the executive branch, in coordination with the legislature and civil society, bringing together technical teams from several departments (environment, housing, mobility) and community representatives, with diversity in gender, race, and age.

In parallel, you set a clear timeline covering every stage — meetings, workshops, public hearings — and widely publicize the communication channels. Events must happen at accessible times and in places easy to reach by public transport. Without this base, public participation becomes a formality, and the plan loses legitimacy from the outset.

## Stage 2 — Reading the Territory: seeing the real city

Here the municipality identifies its features, challenges, and potential. You gather the **basic and complementary surveys** — land use, infrastructure, risk areas, population density, sanitation, and environmental networks — all georeferenced, because it is the map that lets you say **where** each problem actually happens.

The data-driven technical reading has to be cross-checked with a **community reading**: the lived experience of those who inhabit the city. From that exchange comes the first summary table, with the main issues listed and located by area of occurrence — urban, peri-urban, rural, or natural. It is the difference between planning the city on paper and the city that actually exists.

## Stage 3 — Drafting the Proposals: from problem to strategy

With problems mapped, you define how to tackle them. It begins with analyzing the **structuring systems** that connect the city — mobility, sanitation, green areas, public facilities, and centralities.

For each issue identified in the previous stage, a set of **strategies** is agreed upon with society, working as action guidelines. Only then do you select the **planning instruments** best suited to carry them out — ZEIS, charged building rights, Macrozoning — filling in the Proposals Summary Table. Order matters: an instrument without a clear strategy becomes a dead letter in the law.

## Stage 4 — Consolidation: turning the proposal into a bill

This is where the proposal takes legal shape. The steering group performs a **cross-reading** between the desired functions for the territory (Stage 2) and the proposed strategies and instruments (Stage 3), checking for contradictions and ensuring coherence.

Citing the legal instruments is not enough: the technical team must **detail how each one will work**, what will be regulated within the plan itself, and what will depend on specific future laws. It pays to prioritize what the municipality genuinely has the technical and operational capacity to implement. Then you structure the bill's minimum content — principles and guidelines, territorial organization, inducement instruments, and the democratic management system.

## Stage 5 — Approval, implementation, and monitoring

Consolidation ends the drafting, but not the cycle. The bill goes to the **City Council**, where it must be debated and voted on — and where social mobilization has to continue, to keep the proposal from being watered down.

Once enacted, **implementation** follows: structuring governance, issuing regulatory decrees, and, above all, ensuring the plan's actions and deadlines are reflected in the budget (the multi-year plan and budget guidelines law). Finally comes **monitoring** — indicators and targets tracked alongside management councils, feeding corrections and preparing the future revisions, which are mandatory at least every ten years.

## What holds the five stages together

None of these stages stands without an up-to-date, georeferenced cartographic base. It is what locates problems during the reading of the territory, structures Macrozoning and Zoning, and brings transparency once the data is published in open formats.
